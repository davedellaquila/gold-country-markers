Reading the Roadside

The Gold Country Markers Catalogue — every historical marker in eight Mother Lode counties, found, read, and pinned to the map.

Bronze state plaques, red-shirt E Clampus Vitus markers, Native Sons monuments, county plaques, and a long tail of Forest Service and local-society panels — 1,305 of them now sit in one catalogue, each with its exact location and the words on its face. Of the 1,376 listings reviewed, not one was waved through: every entry was either catalogued or written down as skipped, with the reason kept.

More than a third of the roadside story is told by “other organizations” — the Forest Service, state parks, historical societies, and park interpretive panels. The state’s own bronze landmarks are just 154 of 1,305.

§3State landmark coverage

California Historical Landmarks, county by county: how many of the landmark numbers on the state’s list (the California Office of Historic Preservation) carry a plaque in the catalogue. Three plaques the catalogue holds — CHL 706 in El Dorado, CHL 1051 in Placer, and CHL 1053 in Tuolumne — carry numbers the derived state lists had missed; each was verified against the state’s own pages.

§4Nothing waved through

Every one of the 1,376 listings reviewed across the eight counties, accounted for:

  • Staged — 1305
  • Replaced — successor staged — 18
  • War memorials — 28
  • Reported missing — 15
  • Permanently removed — 6
  • Duplicate page — 1
  • Not historical markers — 3

Plaque text came home nearly whole: 1,261 of 1,305 records carry the inscription verbatim (96.6%), and 44 are partial — a transcription with gaps, kept honest rather than completed by guesswork. Coordinates are page-verified for 1,304 of 1,305; the single exception is a Lincoln Highway marker at Big Bend, which stands approximate exactly as its contributor states it, and is flagged so.

§7The gap ledger

What could not be catalogued, kept in the open. A gap here means no photographed plaque record exists to draw from — not that the landmark isn’t real. These are flagged, never filled in.

Amador County — six state landmarks with no plaque page

Middle Bar (No. 36), Kirkwood’s (No. 40), Big Bar (No. 41), Jackson Gate (No. 118), Plymouth (No. 470), and Old Emigrant Road (No. 662) stand on the state list, but no plaque page exists for any of them.

Calaveras County — nine numbers not covered

Seven gaps and two partials. Big Bar (No. 41) appears here too — it straddles the Mokelumne River between the two counties and is a gap from both sides. The state plaque for Douglas Flat (No. 272) is itself reported missing; the site is covered by an E Clampus Vitus plaque instead.

El Dorado County — the drowned towns

Four Folsom Lake sites — Mormon Island, Negro Hill, Salmon Falls, and Condemned Bar — shared one combined plaque that has been missing for decades. Moore’s/Riverton’s plaque was stolen; Marshall’s Blacksmith Shop has no marker at all, just a chimney standing on private land; and Friday’s Station physically stands across the line in Nevada.

Nevada County — one gap

The Lotta Crabtree house in Grass Valley (No. 293): no plaque page documents it. Flagged for a future photo check — the house still stands, next door to the Lola Montez site.

Tuolumne County — two partials, no gaps

St. James Episcopal in Sonora and the Wells Fargo building in Chinese Camp are covered only on other markers’ pages; the Chinese Camp building was designated in 1934 and apparently never plaqued.

Sierra, Mariposa, and Placer — clean

Every state landmark on the list for these three counties carries a staged plaque.

§8How this was built

The catalogue was compiled from the Historical Marker Database (HMdb) — coordinates and plaque transcriptions, each marker’s own page opened and read — cross-checked against the California Office of Historic Preservation’s landmark lists. Every coordinate is page-verified; nothing was estimated or filled in. Compiled November 16, 2026; awaiting its permanent home in Dave’s locations database.
